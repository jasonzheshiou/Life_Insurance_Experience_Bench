Act 1 · The task

Given an A/E table, the model must name three things

1

Which benefit?

Death, CI, TPD or IP

2

Which period?

The years where the change happens, e.g. 2018–2024

3

Which pattern?

One word, plus the direction. Three words are used in these four cases: drift, shock, volatility.

The three patterns shown in these cases

📈 Drift

A sustained change over two years or more. The value may come back at the end. It is still a drift.

⚡ Shock

A sudden change of level with no build-up. Often a single year.

〰️ Volatility

More scatter from year to year. The average level does not change. People often call this a spike.

The shapes above are illustrations of each pattern, not data from the study. The benchmark has one more word, recovery: the IP claim termination rate changes for one diagnosis, and the incidence does not. No case in this talk shows it.

Keep this simple: three questions, three words here. These A/E books are generated from exact mathematical definitions, so we always know the right answer.

The words are fixed: drift, shock, volatility, recovery or other. The direction is increase, decrease or dispersion. "up" and "rise" are not accepted.

A planted change that no finding matches.

Ask the room to score each answer before you click. Answer B is the first lesson: a different start year still counts, because the rule is overlap. Answer C is the second lesson: the scorer is a word match, so "up" costs the model twice. This is why the answer format is fixed.
Act 1 · The task · two measures

Why we must measure false alarms, not only accuracy

Accuracy

hits ÷ planted changes
Did it find what was there?

False alarms per claim

false alarms ÷ findings that counted
Findings that counted = hits + false alarms. When it spoke, how often was it wrong?

Example: one book with one planted change (Death drift). Choose a model behaviour.

Accuracy
False alarms per claim

Why it matters

  • A model can reach 100 % accuracy by flagging everything. Accuracy alone rewards that.
  • Every false alarm is a finding that a person must check and reject.
  • Too many false alarms, and people stop trusting — or stop reading — the flags.

So we always report both numbers together.

The flag-everything example is simple arithmetic: 4 lines × 4 patterns = 16 findings, 1 is right, 15 are false alarms. It finds everything and is useless. The silent model never raises a false alarm and finds nothing. We need both numbers.

What the harness worked out before the model was called

  • The average A/E for each benefit line, and whether that line is going up, going down or staying level.
  • The same question for the last three years, so a late change is not hidden by the ten-year picture.
  • The biggest single year, and the biggest jump from one year to the next.
  • Whether the years move around more than claim numbers alone can explain. If they do, the line is noisy, and one high year proves less.
  • How many years in a row sat below the expected level, and how many sat above it.
  • Whether one big year is bigger than the whole move. That is what tells a shock apart from a noisy line.
  • Whether two benefit lines moved in the same years.
  • A first answer for each line, which the model must agree with or change.

The same page of numbers on every run, from the same code. The model only reads them. It may ask for at most two small calculations of its own: Qwen 3.8 asked for one in 17 of its 72 answers, Qwen 3.6 never asked.

The evidence changed the result, not the model

Without a harness the model must think of the scatter test itself. Qwen 3.6 never did. It saw the spike and called it a shock, three times. With a harness the scatter test is already on the page.

Same model, two results

Qwen 3.6 went from 0 of 3 to 3 of 3 on this book. Only the harness changed.

Every false alarm here is one mistake

It is the extra CI shock for 2022–2023. Nothing was planted there. One wrong word costs the model twice: a miss on the volatility and a false alarm on the shock.

Open the first row: wrong every time, with high confidence. Then the 3.6-harness row: right every time once the scatter test is given to it. The pack prints "empirical scatter 8x the Poisson expectation, Poisson null invalid on this line". That one line is the difference between 0 of 3 and 3 of 3.

Act 1 · the pattern behind the four cases

Reading the chart was not the hard part

In all four cases the models read the data correctly. What failed is the decision about what deserves to be written down.

A large one-year change gets found

On shock books, both harnessed models found 100 % of what was planted, with few false alarms. If your worry is "will it miss the obvious", the answer here is no.

A small multi-year drift gets missed

On drift books, 2 of 3 planted drifts were found — the same before the harness and after it. In Case 3 nobody found the Death drift, in 18 runs.

Extra lines cost the most

Most errors were not inventions. The model named a real movement and used the wrong word, or wrote down a line that was fine. For Qwen 3.8 with its harness, 31 of 69 false alarms were inventions; for Qwen 3.6, 6 of 50.

Four cases are a story, not a measurement. Next: the same test run on 24 unseen books, with a known answer for every book.

Bridge slide. Say plainly that these four books were chosen to teach, not to measure. The measurement comes next. If someone challenges the small drift: agree with them. That is the point of Case 3.

Act 2 · The agentic part

The harness grew one failure at a time

A third model, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, ran this loop for Qwen 3.8. It is not one of the two models under test, so no model tuned its own exam. Each version fixed one failure the last version had shown, on camera.

The growth of the Qwen 3.8 harness · score on the 23 tuning books, one run per book

Read the height as the score and the circles as the versions. Two versions cleared the score bar and still failed, because they wrote too many extra lines. Those were reverted, not adjusted. The last circle is the version that sat the exam.

The loop it worked in

Run the current harness on the 23 tuning books. Keep every question, answer, and score.
For each failure, state why it failed, from the record. Not from opinion.
Change one thing: one statistic, one rule, or one prompt. Never two at the same time.
Rebuild all 23 evidence packs. Confirm that solved books did not change. Run the leak scan.
Re-run the affected book three times.
Compare with the pass bar set in advance. A failed change is reverted, not adjusted.
Save the code and prompt with a fingerprint, so every number traces to the harness that made it.
Never tune on the exam books. The exam is used once.

It cannot make a small drift visible. On drift books, 2 of 3 planted drifts were found without the harness, and the same 2 of 3 with it. In one book a Death drift of about +0.02 a year over four years was found by nobody, in 18 runs.

It cannot fix the hard shapes. Noise traps stayed at 1 of 2 found at best. A change that hits several lines at once stayed the hardest type: 44 % found for Qwen 3.6, 56 % for Qwen 3.8.

Act 2 · What this experiment found

Four findings, before the numbers

1The models read the data well. What fails is the decision about what deserves to be written down.

2What sits around the model is part of the system, not packaging. For Qwen 3.8 it stopped many findings that were wrong. For Qwen 3.6 it helped the model find changes it had missed. Neither gain carried across to the other model.

3A score on the tuning books flatters the exam. Qwen 3.8: 98 % tuning, 69 % unseen. Qwen 3.6: 96 % tuning, 63 % unseen. For both models, the tuning score was about a third too high.

4Cost is part of the answer. The cheaper model found about 93 % as much, in about a quarter of the time.

The four cases and the 24-book exam are the evidence for those four lines.

Give the conclusions in plain words. The last line is the honest one: this study compares models and harnesses against planted answers. It does not compare a person with a person plus a model, and it cannot support that claim.

One exam = 24 unseen books × 3 runs, on one local machine, retries included. Qwen 3.6 found about 93 % of what Qwen 3.8 found, with fewer wrong findings, in about a quarter of the time.

Why three runs, not one

A model does not give the same answer twice. On one book, Qwen 3.8 with no harness named the noisy line and the shock in run 1, dropped the noisy line in run 2, and wrote only the shock in run 3. One run would have said it found it, or said it did not.

Repeat every scenario. Report the spread, not the best run.

A wrong finding is not free. Each one is a line a person must open, read and reject.
Do not blend the three measures. A model can find everything and still be useless if it flags everything. The cost column is why this comparison exists at all: time and tokens were logged from the first run.

Five things to remember

1

Agentic coding is only real when it is measured

A loop that edits code against a test it cannot read, keeps or reverts on evidence, and saves the version behind every number.

2

The model found a lot, and invented some

It found 71 % of what was planted. About 4 in 10 of its findings were false alarms.

3

The harness is part of the system

It cut one model's false alarms by 24 points and raised the other's accuracy by 6. It did not transfer. Re-test the agent after every model change.

4

Two failures, two fixes

Misses and false alarms have different costs. Which model is better is an actuarial decision about those costs.

5

Synthetic data gives a known truth

That is what makes measurement possible. Plant the traps, plant the empty book, and publish the failures.

How this deck was made

Two loops, two kinds of time

Both are loops. The shape differs, and so does where the waiting goes.

1 · Chat window: you carry every file by hand

The model cannot open the deck, so you carry it in and back out again. One round trip per slide, all day.

2 · Agentic coding: it works, you review

You give the whole task. It reads the deck and the repo, edits, and checks itself. The loop turns once per task, not once per slide.

Loop 1 spends your attention in small pieces. Loop 2 spends tokens and one review per task. This deck was made in loop 2.

Close on how the work was done. Left: a chat window has no access to the repo, so every round trip needs you: paste the markup and the numbers in, wait, check them against the real file, copy the block back, fix what it invented. The wait is short but it repeats for every slide. Right: an agent has the deck, the docs, the results and the scripts, so it takes the whole task, works alone, and comes back with a diff and a record. You pay once per task, in tokens: about 70 million tokens for this deck, against a chat loop where the same work costs you a hundred short waits.
